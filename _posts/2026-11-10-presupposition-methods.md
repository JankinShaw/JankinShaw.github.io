Today I had a class called Methods in Pragmatics, and I just want to keep a couple of words about one interesting example, or better framed as a *contrastive* example, about the existence of presupposition.

The example revolves around 'recognize'. Imagine when we use the verb 'recognize', we could say: 

(1) David didn't recognize Sally got a new haircut. 

So naturally this presupposes that 

(2) Sally got a new haircut. 

And it would be rather weird to say something like 

(3)* David didn't recognize that Sally got a new haircut and, the fact is Sally didn't got a new haircut.

Whoever who said (3) might be considered as out of mind.

One could also say that 

(4) David recognized Sally 

where Sally was used as a determiner phrase, a noun phrase. And these two construction seems to mean something differently. And we might even need to be cautious about assuming that they both naturally presuppose something

So formally, we have

$$\text{recognize}\,[\mathrm{DP}] \quad\text{or}\quad \text{recognize}\,[\text{that }\mathrm{CLAUSE}]$$

Now, note that so far we're talking about *third* person perspective. What if, what about we change the subject to a *first* person perspective? Say 

(5)* I didn't recognize he was smoking. And the fact is, he didn't smoke at all, and he wasn't smoking, 

which would sound much more natural than say 

(6) David didn't recognize he was smoking. But he was not smoking at all.

It seems that when 'recognize' was used as the verb in a sentence that starts with a subject with *first* person, we're unable to presuppose the same thing as we could do when the subject was a *third* person. First person introduces a sense of susceptibility to whether the presupposed thing still stand.

One of the classmates with Persian language background noted that in Persian, 'recognize' might indicate a *change* of mind status, So, for example, one could say, 

(7) I didn't even recognize you got a new haircut. 

Think about it, 'I didn't recognize that you got a new haircut'. And in this sense, it's the event that's being predicated over by the verb *recognize* does not even to be related with our perceptual apparatus. It’s more about the beliefs. The person might come to realization that *you*  got a new haircut only when told so.  

Now it's the perfect time to introduce a little bit of terminology here. So such verbs like 'recognize' in English is called a *factive predicate*.

So far, presupposition seems to be a syntactic concept, not really semantic. So semantically means referring to what's happening in the real world or possible truth value attributions. Syntactically means that we're able to derive the presupposition simply based on its form. In all the examples that I talked about before, presupposition can be derived by simply analyzing the factive predicate and the most likely presupposed event. 

Testing whether a clause is presumed in this context or not, therefore, does not necessitate retrieving truth values from other sources. This simply means, with our earliest example:

(1) David didn't recognize Sally got a new haircut. 

Example (1) Presupposes that 

(8) Sally got a new haircut. 

This is presupposition because this is what's needed for even making sense of this sentence at all. And this does not matter if the recognize is negated  or not. So even if we say: 

(9) David recognized Sally got a new haircut.

'Sally got a new haircut alone' as a clause is still what's presupposed here. Now, this is interesting because we don't even need to ask Sally or ask someone who knows Sally well or ask Sally's mother to identify if Sally really got a new haircut. The very sentence (1) per se was put in a utopia where purely by syntactically dissecting the structure, which includes but not limited to, recognizing the verb 'recognize' being a factive predicate, recognizing David is the third person, recognizing there's a clause called 'Sally got a new haircut', etc., would be sufficient to dictate that there is something presupposed here, and that is (8) Sally got a new haircut.

Recognize is an interesting verb. We couldn't even get rid of it in interpreting how 'recognize' itself work.
